# Chapter 1: Ghost in the Silt
The elevator descended through the smog layers, and Sable felt the change in her bones.
First, the atmospheric pressure—a subtle compression in her ears, a weight on her chest that her cognitive optimizer flagged as *normalized descent, 0.3% increase per stratum*. Then, the visual shift. Her subcutaneous display mesh flickered, switching rendering modes: Canopy overlay—clean, optimized, ad-curated lines annotating the world—dissolved into Silt rendering. Signal degraded. Static bloomed at the edges of her vision. Dead zones appeared on her environmental map, grey voids where the city's infrastructure gave up.
She hadn't been down here in two years.
The elevator doors parted onto a flooded street. Neon from a pharmacy drone's advertisement bled across black water, rippling with the rain. The air smelled of wet concrete, ozone, and something else—reclaimed water systems, maybe, or the tang of jury-rigged electronics. Her display helpfully categorized the olfactory data: *Silt District 7-C: characteristic particulate mix, non-hazardous.*
She stepped out. Her shoes—Canopy-standard, self-cleaning polymer—were immediately ankle-deep in water that her display couldn't classify as *clean*. She ignored the notification.
*Dae's neural degradation has been flagged by Mnemos automated health-monitoring.* The message had appeared in her morning stream, annotated with corporate urgency icons. *Patient location: Silt Clinic 22, non-affiliated facility. Recommend descendant consultation.* She'd stared at the words *non-affiliated facility*. Dae had lost his Canopy status years ago—another thing she didn't fully understand, another piece of family history that dissolved into static when she tried to examine it.
She'd contacted Ren.
Ren's response had been a single glyph: *acknowledged*. No time. No meeting point. Just the understanding that Sable would come to the workshop.
Sable walked. The Silt unfolded around her in layers of decay and adaptation. Converted shipping containers stacked three high, rust bleeding down their sides, glowing with interior light from workshops and noodle bars. Jury-rigged relay towers hummed on rooftops, data traffic flickering along exposed cables. People moved through the rain with a purpose her optimizer struggled to categorize—not the directed flow of Canopy commuters, but something more organic, more entangled. Her display kept trying to render AR labels for the storefronts: *unlicensed neural services*, *salvage brokerage*, *grey-market pharmacopeia*. She dismissed them.
Ren's workshop was in a narrow alley where the flooding was thigh-deep. A converted shipping container, its metal skin patched with welded scrap, sat on stilts above the water. A single window, reinforced with wire mesh, glowed a steady, warm yellow.
Sable climbed the metal steps. The door wasn't locked. It never was.
Inside, the air was thick with the smell of solder and ozone. The space was cramped, every surface claimed. Workbenches lined the walls, crowded with oscilloscopes showing neural waveforms, blackbox components in various states of dissection, extraction rigs cobbled together from salvaged medical tech. Wires snaked across the floor, taped down with peeling grey tape. In the center of the room, Ren was elbow-deep in an open cranial casing, her back to the door.
She didn't look up.
Sable waited. Rain tapped against the container roof. A fan in the corner pushed the warm air around. She watched her sister work. Ren's hands moved with a precision that seemed at odds with everything else about her—the faded Silt-grade clothing, the outdated neural firmware that left her temple smooth and unmarked, the way she held her body like a closed system.
After thirty seconds, Ren extracted a slender data filament from the blackbox, held it to the light, and dropped it into a solvent bath. Then she turned.
Her eyes went over Sable in a single, assessing sweep. Not a greeting. An inventory. She took in the subcutaneous display mesh, the cognitive optimizer behind Sable's right ear, the faint pulse at her temple where the Mnemos employee firmware identified her to every scanner in the Canopy. Sable felt, absurdly, like a specimen.
"You're early," Ren said. Her voice was flat, stripped of the tonal modulation Canopy conversations used.
"The elevator was efficient."
Ren's mouth twitched—not a smile. Something else. She wiped her hands on a rag already stained with neural gel. "He's at Clinic 22. It's a ten-minute walk."
"I've already scheduled a consultation through my network," Sable said. "A Canopy specialist can downlink at fourteen-hundred. We should get his full neural architecture mapped before—"
"They won't come down here," Ren interrupted. "And he can't go up. Clinic 22 has what he needs."
"What he needs is a cognitive defrag and synaptic realignment. Not Silt palliative care."
Ren looked at her. For a moment, her expression held something Sable's optimizer couldn't parse. It flagged it as *contempt*, but that wasn't right—the emotional valence was wrong, the micro-expression pattern inconsistent with the category. The system glitched, offering a rotating list of alternatives: *grief, fatigue, resignation*. None fit.
"Let's go," Ren said.
---
The clinic was a repurposed warehouse, its high ceilings strung with fluorescent lights that buzzed like trapped insects. The air smelled of antiseptic and stale filter pads. Beds lined the walls, separated by curtains that didn't quite close.
Dae was in a corner bed. His neural degradation was visible in the slackness of his face, the tremor in his hands where they rested on the thin blanket. Monitors above his bed showed waveforms Sable recognized—erratic, spiking, the architecture coming apart in slow motion. Consciousness would be intermittent now. Windows of lucidity shrinking between long stretches of neural static.
Ren went straight to his bedside and sat on the stool there. She didn't touch him. Just watched.
Sable interfaced with the clinic's system through her display. It was painfully outdated. She pulled Dae's records, queried his insurance status—lapsed, of course—and scheduled a remote diagnostic through her Canopy medical network. The system accepted the request, but the estimated time for a downlink specialist was forty-eight hours. She flagged it as urgent. The system adjusted the estimate to forty-seven hours and fifty-nine minutes.
She looked up. Ren was still sitting, perfectly still, her blackbox offline. Always offline. Sable had asked her about it once, years ago. *Paranoia*, Ren had said. *Silt habit*. But watching her now, Sable wondered. It wasn't just offline. It was a deliberate, sustained disconnection. A hole in the record.
Dae's eyes opened.
They were clear. Focused. A lucid window.
He turned his head, slowly, until he found Ren. His hand lifted from the blanket—the tremor gone, replaced by a directed intensity. He grabbed her wrist.
His grip was shockingly strong. Ren didn't pull away, but her whole body went rigid.
"The extraction," Dae said, his voice a dry scrape. "Don't let them—"
Ren pulled her wrist free. The motion was sharp, final. "He's confused," she said, not looking at Sable. "The degradation causes fixation loops."
Dae's hand fell back to the blanket. His eyes stayed on Ren, pleading. Then the lucidity faded. His gaze went through her, through the wall, into some middle distance only he could see. A tear tracked down his cheek. He didn't seem to notice.
Sable's optimizer flagged the interaction: *heightened biometrics, non-standard vocal stress pattern, possible directed communication*. She dismissed it. Degrading neural architectures did strange things. Fixation loops were common.
"We should get him transferred," she said. "I can pull strings. There's a Mnemos-affiliated facility in the mid-stratum that would take him."
"No," Ren said.
"Why not?"
"He's here. It's done."
Sable felt the old frustration rise, clean and hot. "You always do this. You decide what's possible and what's not. You don't even try."
Ren stood. For a second, Sable thought she would say something. But she just turned and walked out of the clinic, leaving Sable alone with their father's failing body and the buzz of fluorescent lights.
---
They ate dinner in a Silt noodle bar, rain streaming down the windows in constant veils. The flooded street outside reflected neon in shifting patterns. A pharmacy drone hovered past, its advertisement for cognitive boosters casting a blue glow across the water.
Ren picked at her noodles. Sable watched her. The scar behind Ren's left ear was visible when she tilted her head—a pale, raised line. She touched it sometimes, absently, when she was stressed. An old injury, maybe. A Silt thing.
"Why didn't you ever apply?" Sable asked. The question had been sitting between them for years. "For Canopy residency. You have the skills. I've seen you work. You understand neural architecture better than half the surgeons up there."
Ren didn't look up. "I'm useful here."
"Useful to who?"
That made Ren raise her eyes. Her expression was unreadable again, a configuration of features that slipped through Sable's categorical frameworks. "To the people who can't go up," she said quietly.
"I could sponsor you. You know that. I have the merit credits. I could vouch for you."
"I don't need vouching."
"It's not about need. It's about opportunity. You could have a clean apartment. Real medical care. You wouldn't have to breathe this air." Sable gestured at the window, at the rain and the neon and the water that wasn't quite clean.
Ren's chopsticks stilled. Then her left hand spasmed. Just once, a sharp jerk. Her eyes went unfocused for three seconds—maybe four. A sound escaped her, low, like a frequency only she could hear. Then she blinked, and it was over.
"Bad firmware," she said, before Sable could speak. "Silt-grade. Corrupts sometimes."
"Let me pay for an upgrade. A full neural defrag. You can't keep running on degraded systems."
"No."
"Why not?"
"Because I said no." Ren's voice was flat, final. She pushed her bowl away. "We should go back. Check on him."
Sable read it as pride. Stubborn, irrational pride. The kind that kept people in the Silt when they didn't have to be. She paid for the noodles with a wave of her hand, her display deducting the merit credits automatically. Ren didn't offer to split it. She never did.
---
Back at the clinic, the night shift had begun. The lights were dimmer. The buzz of fluorescents was the only sound.
Ren went to Dae's bedside. He was asleep, or unconscious—the monitors showed low-frequency waveforms, the architecture in its dormant phase. Ren leaned over the terminal at his bedside, her fingers moving across the interface. Sable saw her remove a data chip, small and black, and slip it into her pocket.
She didn't ask about it. She was tired of asking Ren things and getting silence back.
Instead, she stood at the observation window, watching through the glass. Ren had pulled the stool close to Dae's bed again. She was leaning in, her mouth near his ear, speaking too softly to be heard. Her offline blackbox meant there was no recording of what she was saying. No record at all.
Sable's display, always analyzing, rendered Ren's biometrics through the glass. Elevated cortisol. Suppressed heart rate. Micro-expression pattern consistent with—
The display glitched. Silt interference—a burst of static from a nearby relay tower. The readings dissolved into pixelated noise.
But in the moment before the glitch, Sable saw Ren's face.
That wasn't a daughter at a deathbed.
That was a guard on a perimeter.
