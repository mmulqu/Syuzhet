# Chapter 2: The Suppressor
Ren's consciousness was a broken city.
Not a clean collapse. Not a Canopy-style controlled demolition. A Silt decay—partial, persistent, eaten from the inside by something she couldn't see but could measure. Seventy-three percent. That was the suppressor's degradation rating, displayed every morning on her workshop diagnostic screen in cool, unforgiving numerals. Seventy-three percent of its designed lifespan consumed. Twenty-seven percent remaining. Eighteen months, maybe, before the wall collapsed and the memory it was holding back flooded her neural pathways at full sensory resolution, auto-syncing to the nearest network node.
She didn't calculate the exact days. She knew how. She just didn't.
---
**Morning routine.**
Wake. The taste of static in her mouth, metallic and dry. Not a metaphor. A physical residue, like licking a battery. The suppressor's side effect—neural static leaching into somatic sensation.
Run the diagnostic. The screen flickered to life in the gloom of her container. Waveforms, impedance readings, a 3D model of her own neural architecture with the suppressor rendered as a red, pulsing mass behind her left temporal lobe. *Degradation: 73%. Cognitive capacity erosion: measurable. Recommend professional intervention.*
She dismissed it.
Check the dead-drop. A physical slot in the wall behind a loose panel, connected to nothing, waiting for a data chip from her Mnemos contact. Empty. Good. No update on the extraction schedule. Mnemos would crack Dae's locked blackbox in nine days. Maybe ten if their decryption algorithms hit a snag. The clock was ticking, but it hadn't started screaming yet.
Check Dae's clinic terminal remotely. She'd installed a backdoor weeks ago. The log showed no unauthorized data transmissions. No attempts to access external networks. But there was a new local file—audio, three minutes long, timestamped 03:47 this morning. He'd recorded it into the clinic's ambient mic while the night nurse was on break. His hands barely worked, but he'd found a way.
She deleted it. Not corrupt. Not degauss. Delete. Clean.
Then she dressed, her fingers tracing the scar behind her left ear without thought. A habit. A reassurance. The suppressor was still there. Still holding.
---
**The walk to the clinic** was a study in sensory bleed.
Rain on flooded streets, neon reflections shattering in the black water—that was real. The smell of ozone that made her teeth ache—that might not be. The sound like tearing fabric, except it wasn't fabric, it was something else, a sound she'd heard when she was twelve and that now lived in the static between her thoughts—that was definitely not real. Not yet.
She passed a noodle bar, steam curling from its vents. The scent of broth triggered nothing. Good. Safe.
A pharmacy drone hummed past, its underside glowing with advertisements for cognitive optimizers. *Unlock your full potential!* The words slithered across her visual field. She looked away. Her own cognitive potential was currently being consumed at a rate of approximately 0.05% per month by the device in her skull. She could calculate the exact percentage. She didn't.
The clinic's fluorescent buzz greeted her like a physical pressure. She went straight to Dae's room.
He was awake. Lucid.
His eyes tracked her as she entered. Clear. Directed. Not the vacant stare of degradation. A window.
She sat on the stool. Didn't touch him.
"Ren," he said. His voice was a dry leaf scraping stone.
She waited.
"She was here. Sable."
"Yes."
"She wants to move me."
"I know."
"Don't let her."
"I won't."
A tear escaped his right eye. He didn't blink it away. The emotional processing was still online, even if the control wasn't. "She deserves to know," he whispered.
Ren felt the static rise in her left temple, a low-frequency hum. She pressed her thumb to the scar. "She deserves her life."
"It's not a life. It's a version."
"It's the version where she's safe."
Both statements were true. The architecture of the room held them, suspended. Dae's monitor beeped softly, measuring the collapse happening inside him. Ren's own collapse was quieter, slower, but no less measurable.
His hand twitched on the blanket. He was trying to lift it. To reach for something. The recording interface. She'd confiscated the input devices, but he kept finding alternatives. The ambient mic. The nurse's tablet left unattended. His mind, crumbling, was singular in its purpose: *confess*.
"Stop trying to record," she said, her voice flat. "It won't help."
"It's the only thing that will."
"You don't get to decide what helps her."
He looked at her then, really looked, and for a second she saw the man he'd been—not the murderer, but the father who'd installed a neural suppressor in his twelve-year-old daughter to wall off a memory and called it protection. The symmetry was almost beautiful. Almost.
The lucid window slammed shut. His eyes filmed over. The tear on his cheek dried in the sterile air. He was gone again.
Ren stood. Her left hand spasmed—a sharp, electric jerk. The static roared for three seconds, a sound like a dead channel screaming. She rode it out, breath held. When it passed, she tasted copper.
She checked his bedside terminal. No new files. She swept the room for any device that could record—found nothing. He was clean. For now.
---
**Back in the workshop,** the fragments came.
Not the memory. The edges. The bleed.
She was elbow-deep in a client's blackbox—a standard recovery job, a silt-dweller who'd fried his firmware trying to bypass a Canopy paywall. Her hands worked automatically, extracting corrupted data filaments, cleaning contacts, her mind elsewhere.
Then: ozone. Sharp. Chemical. The smell filled her nostrils, her throat. Her hands froze.
The client's blackbox was inert. No ozone there.
It was in her head. A sensory ghost from the other side of the wall.
She closed her eyes. Saw: blue light on a ceiling. A work terminal's glow. Not her memory. A reconstruction. Her brain, endlessly trying to solve for the missing variable, throwing up images that might fit the edges.
The sound came next. Tearing. Not fabric. Something else. A wet, final sound.
She opened her eyes. Her hands were steady. Always steady. She reached for the synth-alcohol bottle on the bench, took a measured swallow. The static softened. Not gone. Never gone. But it retreated, from a roar to a hum.
She finished the extraction, transferred the recovered data to a clean chip. The client would get back his failed login attempts, his corrupted files. His small, salvageable past. She took his merit credits. He left.
Alone, she ran another diagnostic.
*Degradation: 73.1%.*
It had ticked up. Point one percent. Measurable.
She powered off the screen.
---
**Sable came down in the evening.** The rain had intensified, sheeting against the container walls. She entered without knocking, her Canopy gear shedding water onto the floor. Her eyes went immediately to the workbench, to the black data chip Ren had left beside the solvent bath.
"You took something from his terminal," Sable said. No greeting. Just accusation, clean and optimized.
"It's a client's," Ren said, not looking up from the oscilloscope. "Corrupted fragment."
"Don't lie to me. I saw you remove it."
Ren finally turned. Sable's face was rendered in perfect, augmented clarity—the subtle tension in her jaw flagged by her own optimizer, the frustration categorized and displayed in her pupils' micro-dilation. She was a system reading another system, and Ren was an error code.
"You've always done this," Sable said, her voice tight. "Kept me in the dark. Like I'm still eight years old and can't handle the Silt."
Ren almost laughed. The sound caught in her throat, became a cough. She pressed her thumb to the scar. "You can't handle it," she said quietly. "That's the point."
"What is that supposed to mean?"
"It means some things stay in the dark so other things can stay clean."
Sable stared. The cognitive optimizer behind her ear would be spinning, trying to categorize this, failing. "You're not making sense."
"I know."
Silence, broken by the rain. Sable's gaze dropped to the data chip again. "If it's a client's, let me see it. I have decryption tools you don't. Mnemos-grade."
"No."
"Why?"
"Because I said no." The same flat finality from the noodle bar. The same wall.
Sable's expression shifted—from frustration to something colder. "He's dying. And you're… what? Playing data-hoarder? Protecting secrets that don't matter anymore?"
Ren's left hand spasmed. She curled it into a fist. "They matter."
"To who? To you? Your whole life is secrets, Ren. Offline blackbox. No augmentations. This…" She gestured at the workshop, at the salvaged gear, the Silt. "It's a choice. You choose to be down here. You choose to be… broken."
The word hung in the ozone-tinged air.
Ren looked at her sister—the clean lines of her, the optimized reality she inhabited, the life built on a floor she never had to question. The anger was a clean, hot wire in her chest. But it wasn't for Sable. It was for the architecture. For the fact that Sable could stand there and call her broken and be right, but for all the wrong reasons.
"Get out," Ren said, her voice low.
Sable left. The door hissed shut behind her.
Ren stood in the center of the workshop, breathing through the static. The taste of copper was back. She took another swallow of synth-alcohol. It didn't help.
---
**Late shift at the clinic.** The buzz of fluorescents was the only sound. Dae was crying again—silent, relentless tears that tracked through the wrinkles on his face. He had no control over them. The degradation had left him just enough emotional processing for grief, but not enough neural governance to stop it.
Ren sat on the stool. Watched him. The monitors showed his architecture fraying in real time, synapses failing, pathways going dark. Nine days. Maybe ten.
His eyes opened. Lucid again. A final window, maybe. They were desperate.
"Ren," he rasped.
She leaned forward. "I'm here."
"You have to… let me…"
"No."
"She's my daughter too."
That was the wrong thing to say. Ren felt the wall in her head tremble. The static rose, a wave of noise. She spoke through it, her voice so quiet the clinic's ambient noise almost swallowed it.
"I was twelve."
Dae went still.
"I've had a hole in my head for fifteen years where my mother's last night used to be." She tapped the scar behind her ear. Tap. Tap. "You put the hole there. You don't get to fill it by emptying it into her."
The words hung in the sterile air. A confession that wasn't a confession—a statement of architecture. Of cause and effect.
Dae's face collapsed. Not into tears. Into something worse—understanding. He knew she knew. Had always known. The suppressor had walled off the memory, but Ren had deduced the truth by working around the absence, by reading the shape of the hole.
"I'm sorry," he whispered.
"Don't." She stood. The static was a roar now. She needed synth-alcohol. She needed silence. "Just… stop trying to record."
She left him there, crying without control, and walked back through the rain-slick streets. The fragments followed her—ozone, blue light, tearing. The edges of the thing in the hole.
---
**In the workshop,** she found it.
While she'd been in the clinic bathroom, splashing water on her face, riding out a static episode, Dae had recorded another message. He'd used the ambient mic again. Three minutes and forty-two seconds. Addressed to Sable.
She played it.
She listened, standing in the center of the room surrounded by the salvaged blackboxes of strangers—all those people's memories she'd recovered while her own stayed locked away. Her expression didn't change. But her left hand was clenched so tight the tendons stood out like wires.
When it ended, she didn't corrupt it. Didn't degauss it.
She ejected the chip. Held it in her palm. A small, black rectangle containing her father's confession. The thing she'd spent fifteen years preventing.
She put it in the pocket of her jacket. It settled next to the extraction tool she uses on other people's memories and has never once used on herself.
Then she sat on the floor, her back against the workbench, and stared at the oscilloscope's blank screen.
The suppressor hummed in her skull. Seventy-three point one percent degraded.
The clock ticked.
She was the data diver who wouldn't dive her own data.
But the water was rising.
