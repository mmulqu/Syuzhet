# Chapter 3: Junk Data
Dae's neural architecture crashed completely between scenes.
Not death. Total cognitive failure. The mind gone while the body remained—a biological machine cycling air and fluids, monitored by machines that no longer reported consciousness, only metabolic baseline. Ren was there when it happened. Sable was in the Canopy, attending a Mnemos quarterly optimization briefing. By the time the elevator brought her down through the smog layers, it was over.
The system denied him the narrative space of a deathbed scene. He didn't deserve it.
---
**The equation changed.**
Dae couldn't provide biometric authorization anymore. His blackbox was locked, but now it was a corporate asset awaiting extraction. Mnemos would crack it with their decryption tools. Legal authority. Seven days. Maybe ten.
Ren stood in the clinic corridor, watching through the observation window as a med-tech adjusted Dae's nutrient drip. His eyes were open. They tracked nothing. The monitors showed flat, low-frequency waveforms—the architecture wasn't just degraded; it was zeroed. A cleared site.
Her own diagnostic screen, back in the workshop, had ticked up again. *73.2%.* The suppressor was eating her faster now. Proximity to the end, maybe. Or just time.
She had one other option.
Her own blackbox. The suppressed recording. If she removed the suppressor and recovered her own data, she'd have a copy of the evidence—timestamped, biometrically authenticated, incontrovertible. She could use it to negotiate with Mnemos: give them what they needed to close their liability file on Dae, in exchange for a sealed record. No background scan flags. No corporate litigation. Sable's identity would hold.
But removing the suppressor meant experiencing the memory.
All of it.
Fifteen years of walled-off data flooding her neural pathways at full sensory resolution. No buffer. No filter. She would be twelve again, in the doorway, living it.
She walked back to the workshop through a Silt afternoon that felt like a poorly rendered simulation. The rain had stopped, leaving the streets glossy with reflected neon. A pharmacy drone hummed past, its advertisement for memory-enhancement supplements casting a shimmering grid across the black water. *Remember everything!*
Her hands were steady. They had always been steady.
---
**The self-surgery was not a montage.**
It was clinical. Methodical. A violence performed with absolute competence.
She didn't bother with anesthetic. The suppressor's location meant local neural blocks were unreliable. She'd feel it. That was part of the architecture.
She sterilized the tools first. Her extraction rig—the same one she used on clients, modified with a mirror array and finer manipulators. Laid them out on a clean cloth: cortical probe, micro-scalpel, neural filament extractor, synaptic damper. Her hands moved through the checklist without thought.
Then she sat in the chair, positioned the mirrors, and began.
The first cut was behind her left ear, along the scar. The skin parted cleanly. Blood welled, dark and immediate. She swabbed it. The scar tissue beneath was dense, fibrous. She worked through it, the micro-scalpel humming at a frequency that vibrated in her teeth.
The suppressor was not a chip. It was a web. A filamentary intrusion woven through her temporal lobe, its tendrils fused with memory pathways. In the mirror, she saw it—a knot of biometal and synthetic neural tissue, pulsing faintly with a dull red light. Degradation had discolored it, made it brittle in places.
She began the extraction. Tendril by tendril. Each separation sent a shock through her—not pain, but absence. A sudden, hollow silence where static had been. The wall was coming down, brick by brick.
Halfway through, the memory fragments began to leak.
Not the event. The periphery.
*The smell of her mother's shampoo—citrus and something chemical.*
*The sound of rain that night, gentle against the window.*
*The way the floorboards creaked in that particular spot near the door.*
They arrived as full sensory experiences, unbidden, overwhelming. She gasped, her hands faltering. Blood dripped down her neck. She forced her breath steady, continued.
The final tendril was the thickest, fused to a primary memory consolidation node. She applied the synaptic damper, felt the world go muffled and distant, and cut.
The suppressor came free.
She held it in her palm. A small, ugly thing, crusted with her own tissue. It had lived in her head for fifteen years, eating her cognitive capacity, shaping her life around its absence. She dropped it into a solvent bath. It fizzed, dissolved into grey slurry.
Then the wall collapsed.
---
**The memory did not return in fragments.**
It arrived whole. A tsunami of sensory data with the timestamp of fifteen years ago, biometrically authenticated, pristine.
She was twelve.
She was standing in the doorway of her mother's home office. The room was dark except for the blue glow of Lien's work terminal. The air smelled of ozone—sharp, electric. Lien was at the terminal, her back to the door. Dae stood beside her.
They were arguing. Not shouting. Something worse—low, urgent, words she couldn't make out.
Then Dae moved.
It wasn't dramatic. It was efficient. A hand on Lien's shoulder. A twist. A sound—tearing, but not fabric. Something wet and final.
Lien slumped forward onto the terminal. The blue light didn't change. It kept casting its glow on the ceiling.
Dae stood there for a moment. He checked his messages on his wrist display. The soft chime of an incoming notification. Then he turned and saw Ren in the doorway.
His expression wasn't horror. It was calculation. Assessment.
He walked toward her. She didn't move. Couldn't.
"You didn't see this," he said, his voice calm. "You were asleep."
She stared past him at her mother's body, at the terminal light on the ceiling. The world did not stop. The rain continued against the window. A delivery drone hummed past outside.
Dae took her hand. His grip was firm. "Come on."
She lived it. Not as a memory, but as a present-tense experience. The suppressor was gone. There was no wall. She was twelve and twenty-seven simultaneously, feeling the ozone burn her nostrils, hearing the tear, watching the blue light on the ceiling, knowing what came next—the suppressor installation, the hole in her head, the fifteen-year architecture of silence.
When it ended, she was on the floor of her workshop, blood soaking her collar, her breath coming in ragged gasps. The memory was integrated. It was now part of her timeline.
She vomited clear fluid onto the floor.
Then she stood. Wiped her mouth. Cleaned the wound behind her ear, sealed it with synth-skin. Her hands were still steady.
She had work to do.
---
**The negotiation with Mnemos took place via encrypted audio channel.**
No faces. Just voices in the static.
Ren initiated the contact through her dead-drop backchannel. A Mnemos compliance officer answered on the third ring. His voice was smooth, Canopy-clean, filtered of all silt.
"You have something," he said. Not a question.
"I have authenticated blackbox data of a Mnemos citizen's unlawful death. Timestamped. Biometric lock. Recorded by a minor witness whose neural suppressor has just been removed."
A pause. The sound of keys clicking. "The Dae file."
"Yes. You're seven days from cracking his blackbox. I'm offering you a clean copy now. In exchange, you seal the file. No background scan flags for his surviving family. No corporate litigation. The liability case closes, quietly."
"Why would we do that? We have legal right to his data."
"You have the right to extract. You don't have the right to avoid a public negligence suit. My data proves your automated monitoring failed to flag a murderer for fifteen years. That's a story. This—" She tapped the data chip containing her memory. "This is a quieter solution."
Another pause. Longer. She heard the faint hum of Canopy climate control. "We'd need verification."
"I'll transmit a checksum. You'll see it's real."
"And the witness?"
"The witness becomes a non-entity. Her record stays sealed. She never applies for Canopy status. She stays in the Silt. You never have to think about her again."
A soft exhale. "We'll need the full data stream."
"After the sealing order is confirmed."
"Agreed."
The call ended. Ren sat in the silence of her workshop, the blood drying on her neck. The deal was struck. Sable's life would hold. The Canopy would not learn through corporate channels.
She should have stopped there.
But fifteen years of deciding what Sable knew and didn't know—of being the one who controlled the information architecture of her sister's reality—was its own kind of suppression. A replication of Dae's crime. Protection as control.
She wouldn't be her father.
She prepared the memory for transmission. Experiential format—not a recording to be watched, but a neural stream to be lived. She attached a single line of text: *From the hole in my head. Now it's yours.*
She sent it to Sable's private Mnemos node.
Then she powered down her terminal and waited.
---
**Sable experienced the memory in her Canopy apartment.**
She was preparing for a synaptic optimization session when the data packet arrived. No warning. No context. Just Ren's tag and the experiential flag.
She loaded it.
And then she was twelve-year-old Ren in a doorway.
She smelled the ozone. Saw the blue light on the ceiling. Heard the tearing. Watched her mother slump. Saw Dae check his messages. Felt the calculation in his gaze as he turned.
She lived it. All of it. The suppressor installation that came after wasn't included, but she understood—the hole Ren had mentioned, the scar, the static, the fifteen years.
When the memory ended, Sable was on the floor of her apartment, her cognitive optimizer flashing error codes, her augmented reality overlay shattered into static. She vomited into her pristine sink.
Then the rage came.
Not at Dae. At Ren.
She took the elevator down. The descent through the smog layers felt like falling through strata of lies. The pressure change made her ears scream.
She entered the workshop without knocking. Ren was at her bench, cleaning tools. The fresh synth-skin behind her left ear was visible, a pink, raw patch.
Sable crossed the room in three strides. "You carried this?" Her voice was a cracked thing. "In your head? For fifteen years you had a device in your skull eating your brain and you—you let me think you were just—" She couldn't finish. *Just a burnout. Just a Silt casualty. Just junk data.*
Ren turned. Her eyes were clear. No static. No filter. "Yes."
"Why? Why wouldn't you tell me? Why would you let me believe—"
"Your Canopy citizenship." Ren's voice was quiet, but it cut through the hum of the workshop. "Your career. Your ability to walk through a scanner without being flagged. You think that happened because you optimized harder?" She took a step forward. "You optimized because you never had to wonder if the floor under you was real."
Sable stared. The words didn't parse. Not at first.
"I made sure the floor was real," Ren said.
And then Sable understood. Not intellectually. Viscerally. The recontextualization was a physical shock. Every frustrating detail about Ren—the outdated firmware, the offline blackbox, the Silt workshop, the static episodes, the synth-alcohol, the refusal of help—wasn't dysfunction. It was infrastructure. The cost of making herself the invisible foundation. The filtered-out part so the clean signal could propagate upward.
Junk data.
Sable's knees buckled. She caught herself on the workbench. "All this time," she whispered. "You were… holding the world up."
"I was holding your world up."
The silence that followed was absolute. No rain. No drone hum. Just the two of them in a room where the encrypted partition had finally dissolved.
Sable left without another word. The door hissed shut.
Ren stood in the center of the workshop, breathing the ozone-tinged air. The memory was no longer a hole. It was a fact. Integrated. She felt lighter. And utterly, devastatingly empty.
---
**Days later.**
The workshop's environmental system cycled. Reclaimed water moved through pipes in the walls with a soft, rhythmic gurgle. The sound of the Silt filtered in—rain on flooded streets, data traffic humming through jury-rigged relay towers, the low vibration of a city that wouldn't stop even when it should.
Ren was at her bench, repairing a client's blackbox. Her hands moved with the same steady precision. The wound behind her ear was healing.
The door opened.
Sable stood in the doorway. She was wearing Silt-grade clothing—simple, un-augmented, already damp from the rain. In her hands was a case. Canopy-made, sleek, out of place.
She didn't speak. Just entered, set the case on the workbench next to Ren's salvaged gear, and opened it.
Inside: high-end neural repair tools. Mnemos-grade. The kind that had never touched Silt hardware. They gleamed under the workshop lights, wrong and beautiful next to Ren's worn, jury-rigged equipment.
Sable looked at Ren. Not through an optimizer. Directly.
Ren looked back. Not after thirty seconds. Immediately.
No words passed between them. None were needed. Sable took a tool from the case—a cortical splicer—and set it beside Ren's older model. Then she took a seat on the other side of the bench, picked up a half-salvaged blackbox, and began to work.
Her movements were hesitant at first. Unpracticed. But careful.
They worked in silence for an hour. The rain eased. A shaft of grey light pierced the container's window, illuminating the motes of dust and solder smoke in the air.
Ren's diagnostic screen, forgotten in the corner, blinked once. Then displayed:
```
SUPPRESSOR STATUS: REMOVED
BLACKBOX: ONLINE
FULL SYNC AVAILABLE
```
Fifteen years offline. Fifteen years as a ghost in the system, invisible, unrecorded, legally incomplete. One gesture and she'd be whole. Visible. Real.
Ren looked at the screen. At the words FULL SYNC AVAILABLE.
She didn't press sync.
But she didn't power it off.
Outside, the Silt breathed. The foundation held.
